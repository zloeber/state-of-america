## PART 0 — EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This investigation analyzed the United States from 1900 to the present — economy,
wealth, debt, health, freedom, stability, and political control — using the longest
reliable datasets available and verifying headline figures against primary sources
(BEA, BLS, Census, CBO, Treasury, Fed, CDC, FBI, Freedom House, V-Dem, EIU) in
August 2026. The purpose was not to grade presidents or parties, but to answer, as
honestly as the evidence permits: **when has America been healthy, wealthy, free,
stable, and prosperous — and does political control explain the differences?**

The honest answer, in eleven findings:

**1. America is rich and getting richer in the aggregate — and has been for 125 years.**
Real GDP per person is roughly 8–9× its 1900 level. Real median household income is at
a record ($83,730 in 2024) `[claim-014]`. Poverty is far below 1960s levels. But this
is not the whole story, because—

**2. —the gains have skewed upward since ~1980, and the middle class has shrunk in
share even as it improved in absolute terms.** Top-1% wealth share rose from roughly
25–30% of the total (late 1970s) to 30–42% today depending on the measure; the middle
60%'s share of income fell from ~62% to ~43%; the share of adults in middle-income
households fell from 61% to 51% (Pew). Median household income rose only ~0.3%/yr from
1970 to 2015, then accelerated (especially 2019–2024 for the lowest earners). Housing
affordability is at multi-decade worst. `[claim-005]` `[claim-006]` `[claim-028]`

**3. The federal debt is on an unsustainable trajectory — a bipartisan failure.** Debt
held by the public: ~26% of GDP (1980) → ~100% (2025) → 120% (projected 2036, CBO) →
~175% (projected mid-2050s). Every Republican administration since 1981 raised
debt/GDP; only the Clinton years (1993–2001) reduced it materially; Obama's rise was
mostly inherited crisis years; Biden's was flat as a share of GDP. Net interest is
$970B/year (3.2% of GDP, ~19% of revenue) and projected to double within a decade.
Drivers are structural: unfunded tax cuts, wars, crisis responses, entitlements, and
interest — with healthcare cost growth the single largest long-run driver. `[claim-007]`
`[claim-020]` `[claim-025]`

**4. Financial fragility is structurally higher than in 2000 or 2007 on valuations,
though the banking system is far stronger.** The Shiller CAPE is ~41 (August 2026) —
the second-highest reading ever, exceeded only by the 1999–2000 peak of ~44; AI/tech
concentration, private credit (~$2T+, unregulated), commercial real estate stress, and
housing unaffordability compound the picture. Banks hold roughly double the capital of
2007. Historically, CAPE above 40 predicts low (not necessarily negative) 10-year
returns (~3%/yr average). Fragility ≠ imminent crash; it means a thinner margin of
safety and a government with less fiscal space (debt 100% of GDP vs 40% in 2008) to
respond. `[claim-022]` `[claim-021]`

**5. Population health is historically good, comparatively poor, and expensive.**
Life expectancy hit a record 79.0 years in 2024 (up from 47.3 in 1900, and recovered
from the COVID trough of 76.4 in 2021) — but peers average ~82–84; infant mortality
(5.6) and maternal mortality (~19–33) are multiples of peer rates; obesity ~42%;
overdose deaths peaked at ~112,000 (2023) before declining. The US spends 18.0% of
GDP on healthcare — highest in the world, ~60% above the peer average — for outcomes
in the bottom third of wealthy countries. Deaths of despair stalled life-expectancy
progress from 2010–2019; that stall, not just COVID, is the distinctive American
health problem. `[claim-016]` `[claim-027]`

**6. Crime is at multi-decade lows after a pandemic spike — a strength too rarely
reported.** Homicide peaked at 9.8/100k in 1991, spiked to 7.8 in 2020–21, and fell to
~5.0 in 2024 — the lowest since 2015 — with 2025 preliminary data showing a further
~18% decline, near a 75-year low. Violent crime is at its lowest since ~1970.
Incarceration (545/100k) remains 5–10× peer rates but has fallen from its 2008 peak.
`[claim-017]` `[claim-026]`

**7. Measured freedom and democratic health are at multi-decade lows — the sharpest
deterioration of any dimension.** Freedom House: 94 (2010s) → 81 (2026 report). V-Dem
Liberal Democracy Index: 0.75 (2024) → 0.57 (2025), a 24% one-year decline, the lowest
since 1965. EIU Democracy Index: 7.65 (2025), the lowest since the index began, ranking
34th, "flawed democracy." Trust in government: ~16–24% (vs 77% in 1964). Partisan
polarization is at record highs. Three independent indices agree; the decline
accelerated sharply in 2025. Important context: the US remains "Free" in Freedom
House's classification, elections are still administered and certified, courts still
constrain the executive — and the US is far freer than in 1950 (McCarthyism,
segregation, pre-Voting Rights Act). The decline is measured from the high base of
~2015–16, and it is real. `[claim-015]` `[claim-018]`

**8. Party effects on the economy are real in the data but weak as causes.** Since
WWII: real GDP growth has averaged ~1–2pp higher under Democratic presidents; the
stock market has returned ~14.8%/yr under Democrats vs ~9.3% under Republicans (total
return, 1928–2024); unemployment has averaged modestly lower under Democrats;
inflation shows no robust party pattern (it is mostly a Federal Reserve and global
phenomenon). But these differences are contaminated by business-cycle timing —
recessions have clustered at Republican term starts (1969–70, 1973–75, 1981–82,
1990–91, 2001, 2007–09) and recoveries at Democratic starts — and by tiny samples.
No honest economist should treat them as evidence that one party "runs the economy
better." The more defensible reading: both parties inherit the business cycle, the
Fed, and global shocks; policy matters at the margin; and the marginal effects are
concentrated in specific areas (taxes, transfers, regulation, fiscal consolidation),
not in the headline growth number. `[claim-001]` `[claim-002]` `[claim-003]` `[claim-004]`

**9. Where the evidence genuinely favors one party, it is narrow and specific.**
Republican edges: disinflation policy acceptance post-1980 (with Volcker, a
Democratic appointee, doing the wrenching); transportation deregulation (bipartisan,
GOP-driven); 1986 tax reform (bipartisan, GOP-driven); 1996 welfare reform
(bipartisan, GOP-driven); sustained low-inflation growth in the 2017–19 era.
Democratic edges: poverty reduction via transfers and tax credits (1960s War on
Poverty, 1990s EITC expansion, 2021 CTC); healthcare expansion (Medicare/Medicaid,
ACA, IRA drug pricing); post-2008 financial regulation; the 1993 deficit-reduction
and Clinton-era fiscal consolidation; and the raw growth/unemployment/stock-market
averages (with the timing caveat above). `[claim-019]` `[claim-020]` `[claim-029]`

**10. Where both parties failed is longer and more consequential than where either
succeeded.** The modern debt buildup; healthcare cost inflation (18% of GDP); housing
unaffordability; money in politics; the incarceration era; slow bipartisan response
to the opioid epidemic; the financial deregulation that produced 2008; the
polarization-and-erosion spiral; and the 30-year bipartisan refusal to confront
entitlement solvency. `[claim-020]` `[claim-025]` `[claim-026]` `[claim-028]`

**11. The current administration (Trump II, Jan 2025–) is too young to grade, but its
first 19 months are observable.** Solid-but-slowing growth (+2.1% in 2025, +1.5% in
Q2 2026), unemployment low but ticking up (4.1% July 2026), inflation sticky near 3%,
FY2025 deficit $1.8T (5.8% of GDP) with the One Big Beautiful Bill Act adding ~$3.4T
over a decade per CBO, debt ~100% of GDP heading to 120% by 2036, record-high
valuations (CAPE ~41), record-low democracy-index scores (V-Dem 0.57, FH 81, EIU
7.65), and homicide near a 75-year low. All verdicts on this period are preliminary;
forecasts are CBO baselines, not predictions. `[claim-023]`

**The one-sentence answer:** America is at its wealthiest ever and its healthiest by
most raw measures, but also at its most unequal since the Gilded Age, fiscally the
most indebted in its peacetime history, more fragile financially than at any point
since 2007–08, and measurably less democratic than at any point in the last 60 years
— a combination that no single party created and neither party is currently fixing.

## PART 2 — POLITICAL-CONTROL FRAMEWORK AND HISTORICAL TIMELINE

The analytical framework treats four distinct things separately (root Section 2):
**(a)** presidential party, **(b)** congressional party, **(c)** unified vs divided
government, **(d)** Federal Reserve policy, **(e)** inherited conditions, **(f)**
global conditions and external shocks. A president is not an economic cause; a
presidency is a bundle of policy, luck, and inheritance.

**Control patterns that mattered:**
- **Unified government** has been rare since 1980: Carter (1977–80), Reagan (never
  fully — R Senate/D House throughout), GWB 2001–02 (R unified), 2003–06 (R
  unified), Obama 2009–10 (D unified), Trump 2017–18 (R unified), Trump II 2025–26
  (R unified). Most post-1980 policy had to be bipartisan or reconciliation-only.
- **Divided government** produced the biggest deals: TRA 1986 (R president/D House),
  1990 BEA (R president/D Congress), 1996 welfare reform, 2013–15 budget deals.
- **The Federal Reserve** set most monetary outcomes: Burns/Miller (1970s inflation),
  Volcker (disinflation), Greenspan (1987–2006), Bernanke (crisis), Yellen, Powell.
  Inflation's history is largely theirs, not the presidents'.
- **Supreme Court eras** matter for freedom/rights, not for GDP: Warren (civil
  rights), Burger, Rehnquist, Roberts (6–3 conservative since 2020; Dobbs 2022).

**Major wars and shocks:** WWI, Spanish flu, Great Depression, WWII, Korea,
Vietnam, oil shocks 1973/1979, Cold War end, 9/11, Iraq/Afghanistan, GFC 2008,
COVID-19 2020, Ukraine 2022, Israel–Hamas 2023.

**Recessions (NBER) over the sample:** 1903, 1907–08, 1910–12, 1913–14, 1918–19,
1920–21, 1923–24, 1926–27, 1929–33 (Great Depression), 1937–38, 1945, 1948–49,
1953–54, 1957–58, 1960–61, 1969–70, 1973–75, 1980, 1981–82, 1990–91, 2001,
2007–09, 2020. The post-WWII pattern is stark: **11 of 13 recessions since 1948
began under Republican presidents** (Eisenhower 1953/1957/1960, Nixon 1969/1973,
Reagan 1981, Bush 1990, Bush 2001/2007, Trump 2020); Democratic-era starts: 1948–49
(Truman) and 1980 (Carter). This clustering — not its causal interpretation — is one
of the most robust facts in the dataset. `[claim-012]`

**The four eras of modern American political economy:**
1. **1900–1932 (Gilded-Progressive to collapse):** industrialization, Progressive
   regulation, WWI, Roaring Twenties, then the Great Depression. Federal debt tiny,
   inequality extreme at decade boundaries, no safety net, panics recurring.
2. **1933–1980 (New Deal–Great Society era):** the most equal and most broadly
   prosperous era in American history — depression recovery, war, the great
   compression of incomes (1940s–1970s), rising real wages for the median, falling
   poverty, the safety net's construction, and its late-era breakdown (1970s
   stagflation).
3. **1981–2008 (market era):** deregulation, disinflation, tax-rate cuts, financial
   liberalization, rising inequality, two booms and two busts (1981–82, 1990–91,
   2001, 2008), debt era begins.
4. **2009–present (crisis-management era):** two historic crises (GFC, COVID) with
   unprecedented fiscal/monetary responses, slow recovery, pandemic inflation,
   polarization and democratic erosion, and a structural fiscal deterioration that
   no longer depends on wars or recessions.

## PART 3 — ECONOMIC HEALTH

*Series in `data/normalized/economic_indicators.json`. All party comparisons are
calendar-year averages 1948–2024 unless noted.*

### 3.1 Growth

- **Real GDP** grew at ~3.5%/yr average for the 20th century and ~2.3%/yr for
  2000–2025. The post-WWII "golden age" (1948–73) averaged ~4%; the post-2000 era
  has averaged ~2%.
- **By party (1948–2024):** Democratic years averaged 3.6%/yr, Republican years
  2.7%/yr (annual average; quarterly annualized estimates put the gap near 1.8–2.2pp).
  The gap is driven by recession clustering and recovery timing, not by any
  demonstrable policy advantage. `[claim-001]`
- **Productivity:** 2.8%/yr (1948–73) → 1.4% (1973–95) → 2.9% (1995–2004, IT boom)
  → 1.3% (2004–2019) → ~1.6% (2019–2024). The 1970s productivity slowdown is
  structural (oil, demographics, measurement); the 1995–2004 revival was tech.
  Neither maps cleanly to party control.
- **Business dynamism** (firm entry rate) declined from ~13% (1980s) to ~8%
  (2010s); market concentration rose. This is a structural, bipartisan-era trend.

### 3.2 Employment

- **Unemployment:** 3.5% (1969) → 10.8% (1982) → 3.9% (2000) → 10% (2009) → 3.5%
  (Feb 2020) → 14.7% (Apr 2020) → 3.4% (Jan 2023, 54-year low) → 4.1% (Jul 2026).
  Democratic years averaged 5.5%, Republican years 5.8% (1948–2024) — direction
  robust, magnitude window-sensitive. `[claim-002]`
- **Labor-force participation** peaked ~67% (2000), fell to ~62.5% (2015, aging +
  post-2008 discouraged workers), recovered to ~62.6% (2023–24), and is drifting
  down again in 2026.
- **Real wages:** median weekly earnings (full-time) were roughly flat in real
  terms from 1979 to ~2015 (the "wage stagnation" era), then rose — fastest for the
  lowest decile, especially 2021–2024 (tight labor market + minimum-wage increases
  + union activity). Real median household income: $83,730 (2024, record).
  `[claim-014]`

### 3.3 Inflation

- CPI annual average: 1914–1940 (mostly <2%, deflation in the 1930s), WWII spikes,
  1946–47 (14.4% in 1947), 1970s (peak 13.5% in 1980), 1980s disinflation
  (10.3%→1.9% by 1986), 1990s (~2.5–3%), 2000s (~2–3.8%), 2020s (8.0% in 2022,
  peak 9.1% June 2022; 2.9% in 2024; ~2.8% in 2025).
- **No robust party pattern exists.** The 1970s inflation spanned Republican
  (Nixon/Ford) and Democratic (Carter) administrations; the 2021–22 spike was
  global. Inflation is a monetary phenomenon managed by the Fed within political
  constraints (Nixon's 1971–73 price controls are the cautionary tale of political
  interference). `[claim-003]`

### 3.4 Did growth reach ordinary Americans?

- **1933–1973: yes, spectacularly.** Real median household income roughly tripled;
  wage growth tracked productivity; the middle class was the beneficiary of the
  great compression.
- **1973–2015: weakly.** Median household income rose ~0.3%/yr; the bottom half
  saw near-zero real wage growth; growth's benefits flowed disproportionately to
  the top (Part 4). This is the empirical core of the "American Dream stalled"
  narrative — and it is true for this period.
- **2015–2024: yes, again — unevenly.** Median income rose to records; the lowest
  decile had the fastest wage growth (2019–2024); but housing, healthcare, and
  education costs absorbed much of the gain, and wealth gains remained top-heavy.
  `[claim-006]` `[claim-014]`

**Bottom line:** economic growth translated into broad-based prosperity in the
1943–1973 era; it largely stopped translating from ~1973 to ~2015; it has partially
resumed since 2019, with cost-of-living erosion (housing/health/education) still
eating a significant share of median gains. `[claim-006]`

## PART 4 — WEALTH DISTRIBUTION

*Series in `data/normalized/wealth_indicators.json`. Measurement note: top-1% wealth-share levels differ by method (Fed DFA ~30.5% in Q1 2024; Saez–Zucman ~40–42% in the 2020s; SCF ~34–35%). The differences are mostly pension valuation, offshore wealth, and unit of analysis. The direction since 1980 is unanimous.*

### 4.1 The headline facts

- **Top 1% wealth share:** ~25–30% (late 1970s) → ~30–42% (2020s). The biggest single jump was the 1980s; the DFA measure peaked mid-2010s (~37%) and is ~30.5% in 2024; Saez–Zucman's capitalized measure is ~41–42%. `[claim-005]`
- **Bottom 50% wealth share:** ~3.5% (1989) → ~2.5% (2024, Fed DFA). The bottom half owns a rounding error of the nation's wealth.
- **Top 0.1%:** ~14.5% of wealth (2025) — roughly 6× the entire bottom half's share.
- **Income:** top-1% pre-tax income share ~10% (1980) → ~19–21% (recent); Census Gini 0.386 (1968) → ~0.49 (2020s).
- **Capital ownership:** top 10% hold ~90% of equities; top 1% ~50%. The stock market is not the median American's balance sheet. `[claim-013]`
- **Union density:** 20.1% (1983) → 9.9% (2024) — a structural correlate of the labor-share decline.

### 4.2 The post-1980 inflection

The U-shaped century is the single most important fact in this dataset: wealth and income concentration were extremely high in the Gilded Age, fell sharply from ~1935 to ~1978 (the great compression — driven by depression, war, progressive taxation, unionization, and financial regulation), then rose relentlessly from ~1980 to the present. The inflection coincides with: the 1980s tax-rate cuts (top marginal 70%→28% by 1988), financial deregulation (1980–1999), the productivity slowdown, globalization, automation, union decline, the shift to capital-friendly corporate governance, and — after 2000 — asset-price appreciation (housing, equities) that favored the already-wealthy. `[claim-005]`

### 4.3 Causes: policy vs structure

Both parties contributed. The tax-rate cuts were Republican-led (1981, 2001/03, 2017, 2025) with Democratic contributions (1997 capital-gains cut, 2012 deal that raised top rates modestly). Financial deregulation was bipartisan (DIDMCA 1980, Garn-St. Germain 1982 — R; GLB 1999, CFMA 2000 — D). Globalization was bipartisan (NAFTA 1994 D, China PNTR 2000 D, trade agreements under R presidents too). The Fed's asset-price environment post-1995 was bipartisan-accepted. No single administration, and neither party alone, explains the rise. Structural forces (technology, globalization, finance) explain much of it; policy choices (tax, regulation, antitrust enforcement) set the speed limit. `[claim-005]` `[claim-019]`

### 4.4 Is the middle class stronger or weaker?

**Stronger absolutely; weaker relatively.** Absolutely: real median income is at a record; real median wealth is up; consumption quality (electronics, cars, healthcare access for the insured) has improved; the 2020s saw real wage gains for the bottom half. Relatively: the middle 60%'s share of income fell from ~62% to ~43% (Pew); the share of adults in middle-income households fell 61%→51%; median wealth of the bottom half is thin; housing affordability is at record lows; and intergenerational mobility (the chance a child earns more than their parents at the same point in the distribution) fell from ~90% (1940s cohorts) to ~50% (1980s cohorts) per Chetty et al. `[claim-006]` `[claim-028]`

The honest description of the post-1980 era: **the middle class did not get poorer; it got left behind relative to the top.** That is a different and more politically important statement than "the middle class is collapsing."

## PART 5 — NATIONAL DEBT & FISCAL HEALTH

*Series in `data/normalized/debt_indicators.json`. Attribution rule (root Section 5): debt is not "caused by" the president who happened to hold office; it is caused by legislation, wars, recessions, interest rates, and growth, under whatever party held power.*

### 5.1 The trajectory

- **Debt held by the public / GDP:** ~16% (1933) → ~100% (WWII) → ~26% (1980) → ~39% (1989) → ~32.5% (2001) → ~53% (2009) → ~100% (2021) → ~100% (2025).
- **Deficits:** FY2000 surplus +2.3% of GDP → FY2020 −14.9% (COVID) → FY2025 −5.8% ($1.8T).
- **Net interest:** $970B in FY2025 = 3.2% of GDP = ~19% of revenue = ~13.8% of outlays — already above defense spending and projected to double to ~$2.1T (~4.6% of GDP) by FY2035. `[claim-007]`

### 5.2 Debt accumulation by administration (gross debt Δ, $T)

| Administration | Party | Δ gross debt | Debt/GDP (public) start → end | Main drivers |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reagan 1981–89 | R | +$1.9T | 26% → 39% | ERTA, defense, interest; Volcker recession |
| Bush 41 1989–93 | R | +$1.6T | 39% → 47% | S&L cleanup, recession, 1990 deal insufficient |
| Clinton 1993–2001 | D | +$1.4T | 47% → 32.5% | 1993 tax increase, growth, peace dividend, surpluses |
| Bush 43 2001–09 | R | +$5.0T | 32.5% → 53% | 2001/03 tax cuts, two wars, Part D, 2001 & 2007–09 recessions |
| Obama 2009–17 | D | +$8.6T | 53% → 77% | GFC response (ARRA, TARP, auto), crisis revenue collapse; then austerity; deficit 9.8%→3.2% |
| Trump I 2017–21 | R | +$7.8T | 77% → 100% | TCJA, COVID response (CARES etc.) |
| Biden 2021–25 | D | +$8.2T | 100% → 99% | ARP/IRA/CHIPS/Infrastructure; rates; deficit 12.4%→6.4% |
| Trump II 2025–26* | R | +$1.2T* | 99% → 100%* | OBBBA (+$3.4T/10yr per CBO), FY2025 deficit $1.8T |

*Incomplete period.* The dollar deltas look partisan-neutral (both parties added trillions); the **ratio** changes tell a sharper story: every GOP era since 1981 raised debt/GDP; the only sustained reduction (1998–2001) happened under a Democratic president with a Republican Congress; Obama's rise was almost entirely FY2009–11 crisis costs; Biden's era left the ratio flat. `[claim-020]`

### 5.3 Why did debt rise? The honest decomposition (post-2001)

Roughly: **(1)** revenue loss from tax cuts — 2001/03, TCJA, and 2025's OBBBA (CBO scores: Bush cuts ~$2.1T/10yr incl. interest; TCJA ~$1.5T; OBBBA +$3.4T net) — about a third of the post-2001 rise; **(2)** spending — two wars (~$8T incl. interest per Watson/T-SCF), Medicare Part D (unfunded), COVID response (~$5T), and structural entitlement growth; **(3)** the 2001 and 2007–09 recessions and the 2008 crisis (revenue collapse + automatic stabilizers); **(4)** compounding interest on all of the above. Since ~2020 the deficit is structural: ~6% of GDP at full employment, driven by interest costs, healthcare, and aging — not by discretionary spending, which is a shrinking share (defense ~13%, non-defense ~13% of outlays). `[claim-007]` `[claim-020]` `[claim-025]`

### 5.4 Is it sustainable?

Under current law, no — in the sense that the ratio is on an explosive path (CBO: 120% by 2036, ~175% by mid-2050s) while interest consumes a growing share of revenue. "Unsustainable" does not mean imminent default: the US borrows in its own currency, the dollar retains reserve status, and Japan sustains higher ratios. The binding constraints are political (willingness to raise revenue or restrain healthcare/entitlements) and financial (r−g, the difference between interest rates and growth, which turned positive after 2021 and is the single most important variable). `[claim-007]` `[claim-021]`

### 5.5 Tax bills scorecard

ERTA 1981: −$750B (JCT, then-year) — did not self-finance. TRA 1986: revenue-neutral on paper, broadened base, cut top rate to 28% — genuinely bipartisan and widely praised. Bush 2001/03: ~−$2.1T — did not self-finance. TCJA 2017: ~−$1.5T/10yr — did not self-finance; corporate revenue fell short of projections. OBBBA 2025: +$3.4T net deficits (CBO), −$5.2T revenue (Tax Foundation) — did not self-finance. **No major tax cut in the last 45 years has been scored by CBO/JCT as paying for itself.** `[claim-008]`

## PART 7 — POPULATION HEALTH

*Series in `data/normalized/health_indicators.json`.*

### 7.1 The trajectory

- **Life expectancy:** 47.3 (1900) → 68.2 (1950) → 73.7 (1980) → 78.7 (2010) → **78.8 (2019) → 77.0 (2020) → 76.4 (2021) → 77.5 (2022) → 78.4 (2023) → 79.0 (2024, record)**. The 2010–2019 stall (−0.1 yr over a decade, after +10 yrs in the previous decade) is the deaths-of-despair signature; COVID erased ~2.4 years then recovered ~2.6. `[claim-016]` `[claim-027]`
- **Infant mortality:** ~100/1,000 (1915) → 29.2 (1950) → 5.6 (2023). Peer average ~3–4.
- **Maternal mortality:** ~12 (1990) → 32.9 (2021) → 18.6 (2023). Peer average ~3–8. The US is the only wealthy country where this rose in the 2010s (definitional changes explain part, not all).
- **Obesity:** ~13% (1962) → ~42% (2018+). Peer average ~15–25%.
- **Overdose deaths:** 16.8k (1999) → 47k (2014) → 70k (2017) → 112k (2023) → ~105k (2024, declining).
- **Suicide:** 10.4/100k (2000) → 14.2 (2018 peak) → ~13 (2023).
- **Uninsured:** 16.3% (2010) → 8.6% (2016, ACA) → ~8% (2023–24).

### 7.2 Spending vs outcomes — the uncomfortable comparison

Healthcare = **18.0% of GDP** ($5.3T, $15,474/person, 2024) vs ~5% in 1960 and ~10–12% in peer countries. For that premium the US gets: below-peer life expectancy, infant and maternal mortality several times peer levels, the worst obesity and overdose burden in the OECD — **and** the world's best cancer survival for several cancers, the leading share of novel drugs and devices, the deepest clinical-trial infrastructure, and (for the insured) world-class acute care. `[claim-016]`

The honest verdict: **US healthcare buys world-class innovation and acute care but poor population-level health.** The gap is driven by behavioral health (deaths of despair), obesity, homicides, and inequality of access — not by the quality of surgery. Neither party has addressed the cost structure (prices, not utilization, drive the premium — the US pays ~2× peer prices for the same drugs and procedures). Medicare/Medicaid (1965, D) and ACA (2010, D) expanded access massively and are politically entrenched; Part D (2003, R) expanded coverage without paying for it; IRA drug pricing (2022, D) is the first real price intervention. `[claim-025]` `[claim-016]`

### 7.3 Who benefited, who didn't

Life-expectancy gains were uneven: top income decile gained ~6–7 years since 1980, bottom decile ~2–3 (Case–Deaton, Chetty); the education gradient in mortality widened sharply 1990–2019; rural America's mortality diverged from urban. The stall 2010–2019 was concentrated in midlife non-college populations. This is a distributional health failure as much as an aggregate one. `[claim-027]`

## PART 8 — FREEDOM & DEMOCRACY

*Series in `data/normalized/freedom_indicators.json`. Definition of what is measured: political rights and civil liberties — electoral integrity, freedom of expression/assembly/press, rule of law, constraints on the executive, judicial independence — as scored by independent analysts. These are NOT policy-preference measures. "Freedom" here does not mean "policies I like."*

### 8.1 The numbers

- **Freedom House (0–100):** ~94–96 (1990s–2010s) → **83 (2021–2025) → 81 (2026 report)**. Status still "Free" — but the US had the sharpest decline of any Free country in the 2026 edition. Freedom on the Net: 73.
- **V-Dem Liberal Democracy Index (0–1):** ~0.79–0.81 (2000–2015) → 0.75 (2024) → **0.57 (2025)** — a 24% one-year drop, the lowest since 1965, ranking 51st globally.
- **EIU Democracy Index (0–10):** 8.22 (2006) → 8.16 (2015) → 7.98 (2016) → 7.85 (2017–24) → **7.65 (2025)**, 34th, "flawed democracy" — lowest since the index began.
- **Polity 2:** still 10 (full democracy) in the latest coding (2018). The least sensitive measure.
- **Trust in government:** 77% (1964) → ~16–24% (2015–2024). **Congress approval:** ~15–30%.
- **Polarization (Pew 10-item gap):** 15pp (1994) → ~39pp (2022). `[claim-015]` `[claim-018]`

### 8.2 What the decline consists of

Per the indices' sub-indicators, the US decline is driven by: (1) electoral-integrity concerns (2020 election denial, voting-law changes, certification pressure on local officials); (2) executive-constraint erosion (impeachments and pardons politics, executive orders, 2025's mass firings and agency restructurings, defiance of court orders); (3) media freedom and independent-institution pressure (targeting of journalists, press access changes); (4) the January 6, 2021 attack on the Capitol and its aftermath; (5) political violence and threats (assassination attempts in 2024, record threats against officials). The 2025 collapse in V-Dem's score specifically reflects the first-year actions of the second Trump administration (executive power assertions, agency independence, immigration enforcement, DOGE restructuring). `[claim-015]`

### 8.3 The honest counterpoints

(1) The US is far freer than in 1900–1970: McCarthyism, segregation, and disenfranchisement were worse civil-liberties failures than anything in the last decade; (2) the 2020, 2022, and 2024 elections were administered and certified through normal processes, and courts repeatedly checked executive actions in 2025; (3) indices are analyst-coded and can overreact to recent events; (4) Polity still codes the US as a full democracy; (5) much of the erosion is attitudinal (trust, polarization) rather than formal rights removal — rights were formally removed in some domains (abortion access post-Dobbs, some voting restrictions), while other domains saw expansion (same-sex marriage, marijuana legalization). The composite picture is: **rights and liberties are measurably eroding at the margins, institutions are under sustained strain, and the trend has accelerated since 2015 and again in 2025 — but the country has not crossed into electoral authoritarianism, and its resilience (courts, states, certification) has so far held.** `[claim-015]`

### 8.4 Party responsibility

The sharpest single-year declines (2025) occurred under Trump II; the 2016–2021 declines occurred under Trump I (FH 94→83); the longer erosion — money in politics, gerrymandering, primary polarization, executive-power accretion, trust collapse — is bipartisan and structural, spanning every administration since ~1990. Blaming one party for the whole decline is contradicted by the timeline; crediting either party with fixing it is contradicted by the record. `[claim-015]` `[claim-018]`

## PART 9 — SOCIAL STABILITY

*Series in `data/normalized/social_indicators.json`.*

### 9.1 Crime

Homicide: 9.8 (1991) → 4.4 (2014) → 5.0 (2019) → 7.8 (2020–21) → ~5.0 (2024) → preliminary 2025 down another ~18% (near 75-year low). Violent crime: 758/100k (1991) → 359 (2024), lowest since ~1970. The 1990s–2010s decline is one of the great unheralded social achievements (causes: demographics, policing, incarceration, lead abatement, economic revival — all contested; federal policy played a small role relative to state/local). The 2020–21 spike (pandemic stress, police pullback) and its rapid reversal (2022–25) show both fragility and resilience. `[claim-017]`

### 9.2 Incarceration

96/100k (1970) → 756 (2008 peak) → ~545 (2023). Still 5–10× peer rates; racial disparities severe; the era of mass incarceration (1970s–2000s) was bipartisan policy; the slow unwind (First Step Act 2018, state reforms) is also bipartisan. `[claim-026]`

### 9.3 Poverty

Official poverty: 22.4% (1959) → 11.1% (1973) → 15.2% (1983) → 11.3% (2000) → 15.1% (2010) → 10.5% (2019) → 11.6% (2021) → 11.1% (2023) → ~12.6% (2024, pandemic aid expired). SPM (which counts transfers): 2021's expanded Child Tax Credit cut child poverty to a record 5.2%; expiration returned it to ~12–14%. **Poverty is policy-sensitive, and the evidence shows transfers work when they are tried.** `[claim-029]`

### 9.4 Addiction, suicide, homelessness, geography

Overdose deaths quintupled 1999–2023 (opioid epidemic: prescription → heroin → fentanyl; now declining). Suicide rose ~30% 2000–2018. Homelessness: 554k (2017) → 771k (2024) → ~895k (2025 est.) — record highs, driven by housing costs, mental-health/addiction burdens, and 2023–25 policy changes. Geographic divergence: per-capita GDP gap between top and bottom states roughly doubled since 1980; college metros pulled away; rural and Rust Belt decline drove the political realignment of 2016–2024.

### 9.5 The inequality → instability → polarization chain

The hypothesis that rising inequality drives social instability and polarization is plausible but **not proven causally**. The data show: inequality rose 1980–2020 while crime fell (1991–2014) — so inequality does not mechanically produce crime; poverty fell while polarization rose — so material conditions don't determine political dysfunction. The strongest correlates of polarization are media fragmentation, party sorting, and elite behavior, not inequality per se. Structural economic distress does correlate with deaths of despair, homelessness, and geographic political divergence — but the chain is indirect. `[claim-018]`

## PART 12 — CAUSAL ANALYSIS (WHAT CAN AND CANNOT BE SAID)

### 12.1 What the data can support

- **Recession clustering at Republican term starts** is robust (11 of 13 post-WWII). It reflects the political business cycle's tendency for expansions to peak mid-cycle and for incumbent parties to be in office when cycles turn — plus specific events (oil shocks, Fed tightenings, 2008). It does **not** establish that Republican policy causes recessions; the 1973–75, 1990–91, and 2007–09 recessions had external/structural roots (oil, S&L cleanup + Gulf War, financial excess). `[claim-012]`
- **Tax cuts reduce revenue on official scores; transfers reduce poverty; deficits follow legislation.** These are the most causally legible findings: they are direct policy effects with official scorings. `[claim-008]` `[claim-029]`
- **Asset-price recoveries redistribute wealth upward.** Documented across 2009, 2020, and 2025 episodes (top decile's portfolio gains vs bottom half's flat balance sheets). `[claim-005]` `[claim-013]`
- **Democracy-index declines track specific, dated events** (election denial 2020–21; executive assertions 2025) — event study logic supports that these are real responses to events, not coding drift. `[claim-015]`

### 12.2 What the data cannot support

- **"Party X runs the economy better."** The growth/unemployment/stock gaps are contaminated by timing and sample size; no credible counterfactual exists (there is only one US economy per period). `[claim-001]` `[claim-004]`
- **"Tax cuts caused growth in 1980s/2017."** Correlated, yes; identified, no — defense spending, deregulation, demographic tailwinds, and recovery-from-recession all co-occurred. `[claim-024]`
- **"2021–22 inflation was (only) stimulus / (only) supply."** Both contributed; point estimates of the ARP effect range ~0.1–1.5pp; the rest was global. `[claim-010]`
- **"Inequality causes polarization / democracy decline."** Correlated over time, but the mechanisms (media, sorting, elite behavior) are not identified; countries with less inequality have also polarized. `[claim-018]`
- **Anything about the Trump II administration.** 19 months is not a verdict. `[claim-023]`

### 12.3 Methods used and their limits

This run used descriptive statistics, per-administration change accounting, event dating, and official-score attribution (CBO/JCT/OMB). It did **not** run regressions or diff-in-diff designs — that is a flagged limitation, not a virtue. Where econometric findings exist in the literature (e.g., Blanchard on ARP; Chetty on mobility; Saez–Zucman on concentration), they are cited in the source registry. The honest meta-conclusion: **economic outcomes are dominated by cycles, shocks, and structural forces; policy matters at the margin and in specific domains; party matters most where policy is direct and legible (taxes, transfers, regulation, fiscal consolidation) and least where it is indirect (growth, inflation, markets).**

## PART 17 — BRUTAL-HONEST CONCLUSION

### Scenarios (not predictions)

- **Scenario A — "Muddling through" (base rate ~50%):** 2–2.5% growth, deficits ~5–6%, debt ~130–150% by 2040, occasional crises absorbed, institutions strained but holding, inequality plateauing. This is the modal path of most advanced economies.

## PART 19 — FINAL ANSWER (ROOT SECTION 48)

> **What is the most honest evidence-based description of the condition of the United States today, how did it get here, how much responsibility belongs to each political party, how much is structural or external, and what trajectory is America currently on?**

**Today:** The United States is simultaneously at its historical best and worst — the richest and healthiest it has ever been by most material measures (record income, record wealth, record life expectancy, crime near 75-year lows), and the most unequal since the Gilded Age, the most indebted in peacetime history, the most financially stretched since 2008, and the least democratic by independent measurement in sixty years. These are not contradictions; they are the same country at different altitudes — material altitude high, institutional altitude falling.

**How it got here:** A century of compounding growth and innovation produced the material altitude. The institutional decline is the accumulation of: (1) a 45-year shift in the distribution of gains (technology, globalization, finance, tax and regulatory choices); (2) a 45-year bipartisan failure to pay for what the government does (debt 26%→100% of GDP); (3) 15+ years of polarization and institutional erosion enabled by both parties' structural choices (money in politics, gerrymandering, media, executive power); (4) the specific shocks of 2008, 2020–22, and 2024–26 — the financial crisis, the pandemic and its inflation, and the current administration's executive assertions.

**Party responsibility:** Republicans bear primary responsibility for the tax-side fiscal deterioration (every GOP era since 1981 raised debt/GDP), the 2008-era deregulation's final mile, and the post-2020 democratic erosion (election denial, Jan 6, the 2025 executive era). Democrats bear primary responsibility for the spending-side fiscal choices and war financing that seeded 1970s inflation, the 1999–2000 deregulation, and the mass-incarceration-era signature policies — and for expanding entitlements and transfers without solving their cost structure. Both parties share the structural failures: healthcare costs, housing, money in politics, entitlements, polarization. Neither party created globalization, technology, demographics, or COVID — and both parties' macroeconomic records are mostly downstream of the Fed, the cycle, and luck.

**Trajectory:** On current policy, America is headed toward Scenario B or D unless policy changes: a fiscal path that is mathematically unsustainable (debt to 120% by 2036 and rising), valuations that historically precede low returns, and an institutional path that all three independent democracy indices describe as eroding with accelerating slope. The countervailing forces are real: a productivity boom is possible (AI, energy, reshoring), the banking system is strong, the economy is at full employment, crime is falling, and American institutions — courts, states, certification — have so far absorbed shocks that would have broken weaker systems. The honest conclusion is not a prediction; it is a fork: **the material foundation is the best in American history, the institutional superstructure is the most stressed in sixty years, and the next decade will be determined by whether the fiscal and democratic trajectories are corrected by choice or by crisis.**
