01 · Executive Summary

The richest, healthiest, most unequal, most indebted, least democratic America in 60 years

What is strong

  • Record real median income ($83,730), record wealth, record life expectancy (79.0)
  • Crime at multi-decade lows — homicide near a 75-year low (2025 prelim)
  • Full employment (unemployment 4.1%), fastest-ever job recovery (2021–23)
  • Banking system far stronger than 2008 (capital ~2×)

What is weak

  • Wealth concentration since ~1980: top 1% at 30–42% (by measure)
  • Debt at 100% of GDP, interest $970B/yr and doubling; deficits ~6% at full employment
  • Freedom at 60-year lows: FH 81, V-Dem 0.57, EIU 7.65
  • Valuations near 2000 levels (CAPE ~41); housing unaffordability at records

Party effects: real on specific policies (taxes, transfers, regulation, consolidation), weak on headline macro (cycles, Fed, and shocks dominate). The debt era and the institutional erosion are bipartisan with asymmetric mechanisms.

02 · America's Historical Timeline

Four eras of American political economy

1900–1932
Gilded → Collapse

Industrialization, Progressivism, WWI, Roaring Twenties → Great Depression (−26% GDP, unemployment 25%). Panics recur; no safety net.

1933–1980
The Great Compression

New Deal, WWII, the most equal era in US history; median income triples; poverty halves; 1970s stagflation ends the era.

1981–2008
The Market Era

Deregulation, disinflation, tax cuts, rising inequality, the debt era begins; two booms, two busts (2001, 2008).

2009–present
Crisis Management

GFC and COVID responses, slow recovery, pandemic inflation, polarization and democratic erosion, structural deficits.

Key structural facts

  • 11 of 13 recessions since 1948 began under Republican presidents — a robust pattern; causation unproven (Fed, oil, financial cycles).
  • Unified government has been rare since 1980 — most big legislation was bipartisan or reconciliation-only.
  • Wars & shocks: WWI, flu, Depression, WWII, Korea, Vietnam, oil 1973/79, Cold War end, 9/11, GFC, COVID, Ukraine, Gaza.
03 · Wealth Distribution

Concentration: U-shaped across the century, rising since 1980

Top 1% wealth share, by measure (range)

Fed DFA: ~30% (1989) → ~30.5% (2024), mid-30s peak; Saez–Zucman: ~25% (1980) → ~41%; SCF: ~35%. Direction unanimous; level differs by method.

Key distribution facts
  • Bottom 50% own ~2.5% of wealth; top 0.1% own ~14.5%.
  • Top 1% income share: 10% (1980) → ~19–21%; Gini 0.386 (1968) → ~0.49.
  • Top 10% hold ~90% of equities — the market is not the median American.
  • Union density: 20.1% (1983) → 9.9% (2024).
  • The 1980s inflection coincides with tax-rate cuts, deregulation, globalization, automation, and union decline — bipartisan policy + structure.

05 · National Debt

From 26% of GDP (1980) to 100% (2025) — and climbing

Debt held by the public, % of GDP

Projection: 120% by 2036, ~175% by mid-2050s (CBO current law). Net interest 3.2% of GDP → ~4.6% by 2035.

Debt/GDP change by administration (public)

Every GOP era since 1981 raised debt/GDP; Clinton's is the only sustained reduction; Obama's rise was mostly inherited crisis years; Biden flat.

Attribution rule: debt is not caused by the president — it is caused by tax cuts (R-led), spending (D-led), wars, crises, recessions, and interest. Post-2001: ≈⅓ tax cuts, ≈⅓ spending (wars, COVID, entitlements), ≈⅓ interest. Healthcare is the largest structural driver.

07 · Economic Performance by Party

The averages favor Democrats — the causality does not

Party averages, 1948–2024 (calendar years)
Why the gap is not proof
  • Timing: 11 of 13 recessions began under R presidents; recoveries under D.
  • Shocks: oil 1973/79, 2008, COVID — none party-made.
  • Sample: 37 vs 40 years; a handful of administrations drive the gap.
  • Fed: inflation and much of the cycle are monetary, not presidential.
  • Where policy is direct — taxes, transfers, regulation, consolidation — party effects are legible. Where it is indirect — growth, inflation, markets — they are not.

09 · Population Health

Historical best, comparative worst, world's most expensive

Life expectancy (CDC)

Peers: ~82–84. The 2010–2019 stall = deaths of despair; COVID −2.4 yrs; record 79.0 by 2024.

Spending vs outcomes
  • Healthcare = 18.0% of GDP ($5.3T; $15,474/person) vs ~10–12% peers
  • Infant mortality 5.6 vs ~3–4; maternal ~19–33 vs ~3–8
  • Obesity ~42% vs 15–25%; overdose deaths peaked ~112k (2023)
  • Uninsured ~8% (16.3% in 2010, pre-ACA)
  • US leads: cancer survival, innovation, clinical trials, acute care for the insured
  • Verdict: world-class medicine, poor population health — a cost-structure failure neither party has fixed
10 · Freedom & Democracy

Measured freedom: multi-decade lows, sharpest drop in 2025

US scores, three independent indices

FH 94→81 · V-Dem 0.75→0.57 (2025, −24% in a year) · EIU 8.2→7.65. These measure political rights & civil liberties, not policy preferences.

What declined & honest counterpoints
  • Sub-indicators: electoral integrity (2020 denial), executive constraints (2025 actions), media independence
  • Jan 6, 2021; record threats against officials; assassination attempts (2024)
  • Trust in government: 77% (1964) → ~16–24%; polarization at records
  • Counterpoints: far freer than 1950 (McCarthyism, segregation); elections still certified; courts still check the executive; Polity still codes 10; status remains "Free"
  • Sharpest declines: 2017–21 and 2025 (both Trump administrations); longer erosion: bipartisan

12 · Wealth & Political Power

Money in politics: the quietest bipartisan failure

  • Campaign spending ~$16B (2024 cycle, both parties) — roughly 5× the 2008 level in real terms; Citizens United (2010) opened unlimited outside money; both parties defend their fundraising machines.
  • Top 1% own ~50% of equities and ~30–42% of wealth; political donations skew steeply to the top — policy responsiveness research (Gilens–Page) finds elite influence on policy outcomes far exceeds median-voter influence.
  • Revolving doors, lobbying ($4B+/yr), and industry-financed campaigns correlate with the policy status quo that produces the report's failures (healthcare prices, housing, tax complexity).
  • Neither party has proposed systemic reform while in power; both benefit from the current system.

Caveat: correlational evidence; the direction of causality (money→policy vs policy→money) is debated. But the concentration of both wealth and political influence since 1980 is one of the report's clearest structural findings.

13 · Fiscal Fragility

The slow-moving crisis: debt + interest + deficits + growth

The deficit is now structural — it persists at full employment, driven by interest, healthcare, and aging, not by discretionary spending (defense + non-defense ≈ 26% of outlays and falling). The US borrows in its own currency and the dollar retains reserve status — "unsustainable" means the path is explosive (r > g), not that default is imminent. Fiscal capacity for the next crisis is thinner than 2008 (debt 40% of GDP) or 2020 (80%).

14 · Financial Bubble Risk

Valuations: second-highest ever

Shiller CAPE (Aug 2026)
~41 vs median ~17

Only 1999–2000 (peak ~44) was higher. Historically, CAPE > 40 has preceded ~3%/yr average 10-year returns — low, not necessarily negative.

  • AI/tech mega-cap concentration (~35% of S&P weight)
  • Private credit ~$2T+, largely unregulated
  • Commercial real estate stress; housing price/income 5.7
  • Government with the least fiscal space since WWII
Why it might be different
  • Banks hold ~2× 2007 capital; 2023 crisis contained in weeks
  • Household leverage moderate and prime; strong earnings
  • AI capex could be a 1995-style productivity boom
  • CAPE is a terrible timing tool
Verdict

Structurally more fragile than any period since 2007–08 on valuations and fiscal capacity, with a materially stronger banking system. Correction not forecast; margin of safety thin.

15 · Party Scorecard

Where each party is genuinely stronger — and where both failed

Republican edges

  • Accepting disinflation's pain (with Volcker, a D appointee)
  • Transportation deregulation; 1986 tax reform (bipartisan, GOP-led)
  • 1996 welfare-reform design (bipartisan, GOP-led)
  • Low-inflation mature-cycle stewardship (2017–19)

Republican failures

  • Debt/GDP up in every GOP era since 1981
  • 2008-era deregulation's final mile (SEC 2004 rule)
  • Election denial, Jan 6 — the largest single contributor to measured democratic decline
  • OBBBA 2025: +$3.4T on CBO's score

Democratic edges

  • Poverty reduction via transfers (1960s, 1990s EITC, 2021 CTC)
  • Healthcare access (Medicare/Medicaid, ACA, IRA pricing)
  • Post-2008 financial regulation (Dodd-Frank; validated 2020/23)
  • Fiscal consolidation when attempted (1993, 2010–15, 2021–24)
  • Growth/unemployment/stock averages (timing-caveated)

Democratic failures

  • Vietnam + Great Society without financing → 1970s inflation
  • GLB 1999 / CFMA 2000 deregulation → 2008
  • 1994 crime bill era; slow opioid response
  • ARP oversize contributed to 2021–22 inflation

Bipartisan failures: debt, healthcare costs (18% of GDP), housing, money in politics, incarceration, entitlements dodged 30 years, polarization's architecture. Structural: aging, technology, globalization, financialization, climate, geography, COVID aftershocks.

18 · The Hard Truth

What the evidence forces you to accept

  • The middle class is not collapsing — it is being outrun. That is harder to fix and harder to admit than a collapse narrative.
  • Both parties broke the budget differently, and neither will admit it. R tax cuts + D spending + shared wars/crises/interest = 100% of GDP. The next president inherits a fiscal emergency neither party campaigns on.
  • Healthcare costs are the national problem — bigger than debt, housing, or inflation — and both parties protect the system's rent structure.
  • America's democracy scores fell to 60-year lows under a Democratic president's watch and fell fastest under a Republican's. The erosion is real, measured, and bipartisan in origin.
  • The economy the campaigns argue about is mostly not theirs. Growth, inflation, and markets are dominated by the Fed, the cycle, and luck. Party effects are legible in taxes, transfers, and regulation — not in the GDP number.
  • Record wealth + record debt + record valuations + record inequality is a new combination. No historical precedent says it ends well by itself; no law of nature says it must end badly. It is a choice.
19 · What Actually Matters

Political vs structural forces — a separation of powers

Matters (policy-legible)

  • Tax rates and their financing (CBO-scorable)
  • Transfers: EITC, CTC, benefits — measured poverty effects
  • Healthcare access and drug pricing
  • Financial regulation and bank capital
  • Antitrust enforcement
  • Election administration, voting rules, executive constraints

Dominates but isn't "political"

  • Federal Reserve policy (inflation, much of the cycle)
  • Demographics and aging
  • Technology and productivity (AI, automation)
  • Globalization and capital flows
  • Energy and climate
  • Global shocks (pandemics, wars, commodity cycles)

The report's meta-finding: elections decide the margins; the margins compound into eras. Party matters most where policy is direct and legible, least where outcomes are indirect — yet the indirect forces (Fed, tech, demographics) have shaped America's last 45 years more than any single administration.

20 · Conclusion

Strong. Weak. Fragile. Resilient. Uncertain.

Strong

Record income and wealth; record life expectancy; full employment; crime at 75-year lows; the world's leading innovation economy; a banking system that passed 2008's lessons.

Weak

Distribution (top-1% at 30–42%, bottom-50% at 2.5%); housing affordability; healthcare value; deaths of despair legacy; fiscal discipline in both parties.

Fragile

Debt 100% of GDP and rising; interest $970B and doubling; valuations at 2000 levels; private credit; democracy indices at 60-year lows.

Resilient

Institutions absorbed Jan 6, COVID, 2020–24 inflation, and 2025's executive era so far; courts, states, certification held; productivity and energy abundance are live upside.

Uncertain

Whether the democracy decline is durable erosion or recoverable strain; whether AI is a productivity boom or a bubble; whether the fiscal path is corrected by choice or by crisis.

The fork

The next decade is a choice between fiscal/institutional correction (Scenario C) and forced adjustment (B/D). The material foundation is the best in American history; the institutional superstructure is the most stressed in sixty years.
